The Tariff Reclassification: How One Code Change Recovers Thousands in Duties

Converse cut its duty rate from 37.5 percent to 20 percent by designing a shoe to fit a different heading. One code change, honestly argued and locked in by a binding ruling, is worth $175,000 a year on a million dollars of imports.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

The shoe looked like a sneaker, and that was the problem. Converse had designed its Chuck Taylor with a thin rubber overlay on the toe and foxing, and the question before the Court of International Trade in 2019 was what the shoe was, in the eyes of the tariff schedule: a sneaker with a rubber outsole, dutiable at 37.5 percent under HTSUS 6404.19, or a sneaker with a textile-dominant upper, dutiable at 20 percent under HTSUS 6404.11. The difference was 17.5 percentage points. On a million dollars of imports, it was $175,000. The court looked at the surface area of the upper, found textile dominant, and put the shoe in the lower heading. Converse had not hidden anything. It had designed the shoe knowing the rule, and the rule rewarded the design.

That is reclassification: the same product, the same shipment, a different code, and a duty bill that changes by six figures. Every imported good is assigned a 10-digit HTSUS code, and the code decides the duty. The classification is governed by the six General Rules of Interpretation, applied in order, and by CBP’s rulings on similar goods — more than 220,000 of them, published in CBP’s free Customs Rulings Online Search System, CROSS. When the code is wrong, the importer is overpaying duty it does not owe, or underpaying duty it does and will owe later, with penalties. When the code is right but nobody has checked it in years, the importer is usually overpaying, because codes are set once — by a broker, a supplier, or a previous employee — and then copied forward on every entry since, without anyone checking them against the current schedule.

The ruling that locks the answer in

The first step is not to change the code. It is to ask CBP what the code is. Any importer, or its authorized agent, with a direct and demonstrable interest can request a binding ruling under 19 CFR Part 177, and the request goes to the National Commodity Specialist Division in New York through CBP’s eRulings template or by letter. CBP says the division generally issues rulings within 30 calendar days of receipt, and rulings that must be referred to Headquarters are issued within 90 days. There is no government filing fee. A ruling binds CBP personnel for the transaction and the articles it describes, at every U.S. port, until it is modified or revoked — and under 19 U.S.C. 1625, a ruling in effect for at least 60 days cannot be changed without a published proposal, at least 30 days for comment, and a further 60 days before the change takes effect. The ruling is the cheapest insurance in customs: thirty days and no fee for an answer CBP cannot take back without warning.

The second step is the recovery, and it has two clocks. For entries that have not yet liquidated, a Post-Summary Correction can fix most data elements, including classification, within 300 days of entry. For entries that have already liquidated, the tool is a protest on CBP Form 19, filed within 180 days of liquidation under 19 U.S.C. 1514. Older misclassification has limited recovery options, and that is the point of the exercise: the refund window is measured in months, not years, and a code that has been wrong for three years is recoverable only for the entries still inside the window. The rest is gone. An importer that finds a misclassification should file the correction for the open entries first and argue about the old ones second.

What the wrong code costs when CBP finds it first

Reclassification cuts both ways, and the other direction is the expensive one. Ford Motor Company learned it in 2019, when the Federal Circuit sided with CBP over the Transit Connect van: Ford imported the van from Turkey configured with rear seats and windows, then removed them in a U.S. bonded warehouse to create cargo vans, and CBP reclassified the vehicles as cargo vans at 25 percent duty under heading 8704 rather than passenger vehicles at 2.5 percent under heading 8703. The gap was 22.5 percentage points, and the court held that the vehicle as imported was the vehicle that mattered. A reclassification that moves the rate up is not a refund. It is a bill, with interest, for every entry inside the window.

The penalty side is steeper still. Under 19 U.S.C. § 1592, CBP can assess penalties based on the importer’s culpability, and can collect underpaid duties going back five years on top of the penalty. For negligence — the failure to exercise reasonable care, which an unintentional wrong code can satisfy — the maximum is the lesser of the domestic value of the goods or twice the lost duties, taxes, and fees; without a revenue loss, up to 20 percent of the dutiable value. For gross negligence, four times the lost duties or 40 percent of the value. For fraud, up to the full domestic value of the goods. A prior disclosure made before CBP starts an investigation caps the penalty sharply — for negligence and gross negligence, typically to the interest on the lost duties. The lesson is not that a wrong code is a crime. It is that a wrong code the importer found and fixed is a correction, and a wrong code CBP found first is a penalty case.

A worked example, and a real one

Take the Converse numbers and run them as an importer would. A million dollars of sneakers a year at 37.5 percent pays $375,000 in duty. At 20 percent it pays $200,000. The reclassification is worth $175,000 a year, every year, for as long as the product ships — and the recovery for past entries inside the protest window is the same arithmetic applied backwards, entry by entry. That is the scale of a single heading change on a single product, and it is why classification reviews start with the highest-volume imports: the duty gap is the rate gap multiplied by the volume, and the volume is what turns a percentage point into a refund.

The real-world version ran through Braumiller Law in 2026. A U.S. importer of industrial dehumidifiers came under CBP scrutiny after a site visit, when an import specialist took the position that a higher-duty classification applied; CBP rate-advanced entries, required the new classification immediately, and threatened penalty exposure requiring a prior disclosure. The Braumiller team, led by Senior Associate Attorney Kerry Wang, argued that HTS 8479.89.1000 expressly covers “air humidifiers or dehumidifiers” with no limitation on size or commercial setting, that CBP’s own prior rulings confirmed the subheading applies to non-domestic, heavy-duty units, and that the importer already held a binding CBP ruling classifying the same product under the duty-free provision. Nearly a year after the Headquarters presentation, CBP agreed: duty-free treatment was restored on all covered entries, the penalty exposure was eliminated, and no prior disclosure was required — because the classification had been correct from the start. The binding ruling the importer already held was the whole defense.

The counterargument: a code is a judgment, not a fact

It is worth saying that reclassification is not a trick, and that the line between a legitimate reclassification and tariff engineering is thinner than the Converse story makes it look. Converse designed a shoe to fit a heading, and the court allowed it; Ford configured a van to fit a heading, and the court did not. The difference was not the intent. It was the product as imported, measured against the words of the heading. A bona fide disagreement about classification is not negligence — courts have held that a reasonable basis for the classification chosen is a defense — but a classification chosen because it is cheaper, without a reading of the heading that supports it, is not a disagreement. It is a gamble, and the penalty table is the price of losing it.

That is why the ruling matters more than the reclassification. A code changed on the importer’s own authority, without a binding ruling, is a position CBP can challenge retroactively on every entry inside the window. A code changed under a binding ruling is a position CBP has already accepted, in writing, for those exact goods. The review should run top-down by volume, the ruling request should go in before the code changes, the correction should be filed for the open entries inside their windows, and the prior disclosure should be ready for anything the review finds that was wrong in the other direction. The duty saved is real — $175,000 a year on a million dollars of sneakers is real — but it is only saved once, and only if the importer asks CBP first.
