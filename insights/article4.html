How Long a Customs Duty Refund Takes — and Where the Claim Stalls

With accelerated payment, a drawback refund arrives in about three weeks. Without it, the same claim waits one to three years. The difference is decided before the claim is filed.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026

Two importers filed duty drawback claims on nearly identical facts — goods that came in, paid their duty, and went straight back out to a buyer abroad. The first importer’s money arrived in about three weeks. The second waited more than a year. The difference between the two was not the claim. It was a single privilege, applied for months before either claim was filed, that one importer held and the other had never heard of.

That privilege is accelerated payment, and it is the whole answer to how long a drawback refund takes. With it, U.S. Customs and Border Protection usually pays the estimated refund within about three weeks of accepting the claim in the Automated Commercial Environment, according to Stable Software’s October 2026 guide to CBP timelines. Without it, the refund waits until the claim liquidates, which CBP says usually takes one to three years: every import entry on the claim must be liquidated and past its 180-day protest period, and a drawback specialist must finish the review. The same claim, the same money, the same agency — three weeks or three years, decided before the claim is filed.

The two clocks, and why they diverge

The fast clock runs on a bond. Accelerated payment is CBP paying the estimated drawback before it finishes reviewing the claim, under 19 CFR 190.92. It is not the liquidation itself; that still comes later, usually twelve to eighteen months after filing, and CBP can still audit the claim after paying. To get it, the claimant applies in writing, CBP decides within 90 days, and the claimant needs a bond large enough to cover the estimated refund — CBP’s ACE guidance asks for an active and sufficient bond on file in eBonds. Tariff Refund Credits puts the same window at four to six weeks from submission to ACH deposit for a clean claim with the privilege approved, and notes the bond must cover 100 percent of the annual drawback projection. The three-week figure is the payment after acceptance, not the time to set the privilege up. Setting it up takes the 90-day application window plus the bond. An importer that applies for the privilege the week it files its first claim has already lost the race.

The slow clock runs on liquidation, and liquidation is where the months hide. CargoTrans, a licensed customs broker, states the statutory side plainly: CBP must liquidate a drawback claim within one year of filing. In practice, the claim cannot liquidate until every import entry on it is liquidated and past the 180-day protest period, and the drawback specialist’s review sits on top of that — which is why Stable Software reports one to three years as the usual span, and why Tariff Refund Credits puts unaccelerated refunds at 12 to 24 months. The one-year rule and the three-year reality are not a contradiction. The one-year rule is the deadline for the claim’s own liquidation; the entries underneath it have their own clocks, and the claim waits for the slowest entry on it. A claim built on fifty entries waits for the fiftieth.

Where the claim stalls

The stalls are almost all documentation, and most of them are set before the claim is filed. The first is the notice. Under 19 CFR 190.35, the claimant must file a Notice of Intent to Export, Destroy, or Return Merchandise for Purposes of Drawback on CBP Form 7553, at least five working days before the intended export, at the port of intended examination. A drawback claim on goods that already left, with no notice filed, fails on procedure before it reaches substance, as DutyWatchHQ noted in November 2026. The second is the substitution analysis: drawback by substitution lets an importer claim the duty on an import by exporting a commercially interchangeable good, but the analysis that ties the two together has to be documented, and most importers keep the entry record, the export record, and the substitution analysis in three different places — if they keep them at all. The records only have to be kept for three years after payment under 19 CFR 190.15, shorter than the five-year claim window, which means the paper trail can die before the claim does.

The third stall is the match itself. CBP refunds roughly $1 billion a year through drawback, and industry estimates put the eligible money left unclaimed at about $15 billion a year — roughly four-fifths of what the program could return. The gap is not a mystery. Drawback asks an importer to connect the entry that paid the duty, the export that took the goods away, and the substitution analysis that ties them together, and the claim that cannot connect all three does not get paid slowly. It gets paid never, or it gets paid after a development letter that asks for the missing link and starts a new clock. The five-year filing window is generous by customs standards — the claim must be filed within five years of import, and the export must fall inside the same window — but five years of eligibility is no help to a file whose export proof was a bill of lading nobody saved.

The practical fix is a calendar, not a better claim. An importer that files drawback claims every quarter should apply for accelerated payment at least ninety days before the first claim it wants paid fast, size the bond to the year’s expected drawback rather than a single claim, and file the Form 7553 notice five working days before every export it intends to claim — not after the goods have left. The privilege, once granted, turns the refund into a receivable the finance team can date: roughly three weeks from acceptance in ACE, instead of a line item that might arrive this year or next. The importers that treat the privilege as part of the import program, set up when the first container ships, never have to learn the slow clock exists. The ones that discover it from a fourteen-month wait have already paid for the lesson in working capital.

The counterargument: speed is a choice the importer already made

There is a version of this story in which CBP is the villain, sitting on a billion dollars of refunds for years. It is mostly wrong. CBP pays the accelerated claim in three weeks because the bond guarantees the money back if the claim fails on review — the agency is not being generous, it is being secured. The unaccelerated claim waits because the agency has no security and no reason to pay before it has finished checking, and the check takes as long as the entries underneath it take to liquidate. An importer that wants its money in three weeks can have it, for the price of a bond and a 90-day application filed before the first claim. The importers waiting fourteen months are, in most cases, importers that never applied. The delay is not imposed on them. It is the default they accepted by not filing the paperwork that changes it.

That argument has a limit, and the limit is the interest. CBP does not pay interest on ordinary drawback refunds — the Court of International Trade held in 2000 that the interest provision in 19 U.S.C. 1505(c) does not apply to ordinary drawback claims, a reading the Federal Circuit confirmed in Performance Additives in February 2026. A refund that arrives in three years is worth less than the same refund in three weeks, and the importer bears the whole cost of the wait. The privilege exists, but it is not free: the bond costs money, the application takes ninety days, and a small importer with one claim a year may reasonably decide the bond is not worth it. The honest accounting is that the fast clock costs a bond and the slow clock costs the time value of the refund, and the importer should price both before choosing. What no importer should do is file the claim first and discover the two clocks second.
