The Ninety-Nine Percent Refund

Duty drawback returns 99 cents of every dollar of duty on goods that leave the country. Roughly four-fifths of the money it could return never gets claimed.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026

On a late afternoon in 1789, the First Congress did something Congress has almost never done since: it wrote a refund into a tariff law. The Tariff Act of 1789 taxed imported goods, and in the same breath it promised that the tax would come back — 99 cents on the dollar — if those goods were later exported. The promise has survived every tariff war, every trade agreement, and every rewrite of the customs code since. It is still on the books, still at 99 percent, and still, for the most part, unclaimed.

Drawback is the oldest refund mechanism in American trade law, and it works the way a good refund should: the importer pays the duty when the goods come in, and Customs pays it back when the goods go out again, or are destroyed under Customs supervision. The statute is 19 U.S.C. § 1313, rewritten by the Trade Facilitation and Trade Enforcement Act of 2015 and set out in the regulations at 19 CFR Part 190. CBP keeps one percent of the duties, taxes, and fees as an administrative charge. The rest comes back — and not only the duty. The merchandise processing fee and the harbor maintenance fee come back with it.

The reach of the program is wider than most importers assume. A claim can be filed for goods exported in the same condition they arrived in, for goods used to manufacture something else that is then exported, for goods that are rejected and sent back, and for goods destroyed under supervision. It can also be filed by substitution: an importer that exports a commercially interchangeable good — the same kind, the same quality, the same classification — can claim the duty on the import even if the exact units that arrived are not the ones that left. That last point matters, because it turns drawback from a warehouse-tracking exercise into an accounting exercise, and accounting exercises are the kind that scale.

The clock is generous by customs standards. A claim must be filed within five years of the date of import, and the export or destruction must fall inside the same window. Five years is a long time in trade — long enough that an importer can look back across a tariff spike it barely noticed at the time and find money sitting in its own entry records. The filing itself is electronic, through the Automated Commercial Environment; paper claims stopped being accepted in February 2019, and the claim rides on CBP Form 7551, filed by a licensed customs broker or by the importer itself.

Five years is a long time in trade — long enough to look back across a tariff spike and find money sitting in your own entry records.

And yet the money stays put. CBP refunds roughly $1 billion a year through drawback, and industry estimates put the eligible money left unclaimed at about $15 billion a year — roughly four-fifths of what the program could return. The gap is not a mystery. Drawback asks an importer to connect three records that live in three different places: the entry that paid the duty, the export that took the goods away, and the substitution analysis that ties them together. Most importers keep none of those records in one place, and the records themselves only have to be kept for three years after payment under 19 CFR 190.15 — shorter than the five-year claim window, which means the paper trail can die before the claim does.

That gap is where the recovery firms live, and it is a real trade now. CargoTrans, a licensed customs broker, runs drawback as a managed service on its Captain platform and offers a free assessment of an importer's last five years of entries — the entire lookback, in one offer. RSK, the tariff and trade practice, files drawback claims in ACE through its own licensed brokers under its own filer code, and its IEEPA recovery program reports more than 7,500 entities assisted and more than $3 billion in recoveries supported. DrawbackHQ works on contingency — no fee until the importer is paid — and puts its average missed refund above $40,000. One Eagle Advisory frames the offer the way the statute does: 99 percent back, five years, a mechanism older than the income tax. These firms are not selling a loophole. They are selling the record-keeping that the refund requires.

The speed of the money depends on how the claim is filed. With drawback privilege and a bond, CBP generally pays within four to six weeks of accepting an accelerated-payment claim. Without it, the importer waits for the claim to liquidate, which can take years — and CBP must liquidate within one year of filing, though the claim itself can sit longer. The difference between four weeks and two years is the difference between a refund that funds the next quarter and a refund that arrives after the people who filed it have moved on. It is also the difference between a firm that knows the privilege rules and one that does not.

What counts, and what doesn't

The eligibility picture shifted through 2026, and the shifts are worth tracking because they decide which duties come back. Section 301 duties are confirmed recoverable through drawback — CBP said so in CSMS message 42421561 — and the Section 122 ten-percent surcharge imposed in February 2026 is eligible as well. Section 232 duties are the moving piece: their treatment has shifted through the 2026 proclamations, and a claim that was good in January may not be good in December. Antidumping and countervailing duties are never recoverable through drawback. IEEPA duties are not recovered through drawback at all — they have their own refund process, and the same duties cannot be recovered twice. A firm that files drawback on IEEPA duties is filing the wrong claim in the wrong system.

That last point is the one that separates the serious firms from the opportunists. The IEEPA refund pool — about $166 billion, per the Congressional Budget Office — has pulled a crowd into the recovery trade, and the crowd includes firms that have never filed a drawback claim. Drawback is the permanent business underneath the one-time event. The IEEPA pool will drain as CBP works through its CAPE declarations; the drawback pool refills every time a container arrives and leaves again. A firm that builds its practice on drawback owns a refund stream that does not expire with a court decision.

The counterargument

It is worth asking whether the gap will close on its own. CBP has been making drawback easier to file for a decade — electronic filing, simplified substitution rules under TFTEA, and accelerated payment for bonded claimants are all steps in that direction. If the filing gets simple enough, the recovery firms' advantage narrows, and the 80 percent unclaimed figure falls toward the level of an ordinary administrative program. There is a version of this market in which the money stops being left on the table because Customs finally hands it back without a middleman.

That version has not arrived, and the reasons are structural rather than technical. The five-year window is long, but the records die at three years. The substitution analysis is an accounting exercise that most importers will never staff. And the duties themselves are getting bigger — the average U.S. tariff rate rose from 2.6 percent to 13 percent during 2025, per the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, which means the drawback on a single entry is worth five times what it was worth two years ago. The pool is growing faster than the filing is getting easier.

What that means, in practice, is that the refund business is a record-keeping business with a five-year tail, and the firms that do the record-keeping are the ones that get paid. The statute has been promising 99 cents back since 1789. The importers who collect it are the ones who can prove, entry by entry, that the goods left. Everything else — the 80 percent unclaimed, the $15 billion a year, the contingency fees — is a measure of how few of them can.
