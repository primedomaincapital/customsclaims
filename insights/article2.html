The Hundred-and-Sixty-Six-Billion-Dollar Refund

On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held that the IEEPA tariffs were never authorized. About $166 billion in duties became refundable overnight — and the refund runs on paperwork, not on the ruling.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026

The morning of February 20, 2026, the finance chief of a mid-size American importer opened her email to find two messages that did not belong together. The first was a wire confirmation: her company had just paid its monthly duty bill, six figures, on goods that had arrived the week before. The second was a news alert: the Supreme Court had ruled 6–3, in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, that the tariffs behind those duties were never authorized in the first place. The money she had just sent was, as a matter of law, owed back to her. The only question was how long it would take to get it.

The answer to that question is the story of the largest refund in American trade history, and it is a story about process, not law. The Court's ruling settled the legal question — the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not give the president authority to impose tariffs — but a ruling does not wire money. About 330,000 importers had remitted about $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries, per the Congressional Budget Office's estimate, and every dollar of it had to come back through Customs and Border Protection's systems, entry by entry, with interest, through a process CBP built from scratch in a matter of weeks.

The process has a name — CAPE, the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries — and it launched on April 20, 2026. The mechanics are blunt: an importer files a CAPE declaration, up to 9,999 entries per declaration, and CBP pays the refund generally within 60 to 90 days after accepting it. Phase 1 covered the easy cases — entries that had not yet liquidated, and entries within 80 days of liquidation. The importers who were ready moved fast. By April 26, six days in, about 75,300 CAPE declarations had been filed, covering more than 11.2 million entries. By October 2, CBP had accepted about $136.6 billion in claims and sent about $126 billion to the Treasury for payment, interest included.

The Court's ruling settled the legal question. A ruling does not wire money — every dollar had to come back through CBP's systems, entry by entry, with interest.

The scale of the refund is hard to hold in the mind, so it helps to set it against the ordinary numbers of the customs system. In fiscal year 2025, the Treasury collected $195 billion in net customs duties — the entire year's take. The IEEPA refund pool is about 85 percent of that. Gross customs deposits in fiscal 2026 reached $332.6 billion, against $215.2 billion in fiscal 2025 and $95.7 billion in fiscal 2024 — the tariff spike, in one line. And the Treasury paid out $157.8 billion through CBP in fiscal 2026, against about $10.5 billion in each of the two prior fiscal years. The refund is not a rounding error in the customs system. For a year, it was the customs system.

Who got paid, and who is still waiting

The refunds that moved first went, by and large, to the importers best equipped to claim them. Judge Richard Eaton noted in May 2026 that most early refunds went to large importers — the companies with in-house customs counsel, with brokers on retainer, with entry records in a single system. The average refund ran to about 1.7 percent of a firm's annual revenue, which is real money at any scale, but it is money that lands differently on a company that can staff a CAPE filing team than on one that cannot.

The stragglers are a catalogue of the ways a refund can stall. About $1.3 billion sat on hold in mid-September 2026 for a reason that has nothing to do with the merits of any claim: CBP did not have the importers' ACH bank data, and since February 2026 all CBP refunds have been issued electronically. Some 20,184 refunds were waiting on a bank account number. Phase III of CAPE, which opened on October 6, 2026, covers the finally liquidated entries — about $11.4 billion, or 6.9 percent of the IEEPA duties — the cases where the entry had already liquidated before anyone sued, and where the importer's only route is a declaration filed late in the process. And more than 3,700 refund suits have been filed at the Court of International Trade, many of them by importers whose entries fall outside CAPE's phases entirely.

That is where the recovery firms come in, and they came in fast. RSK's IEEPA Managed Recovery Program, at ieepa.com, reports more than 7,500 entities assisted and more than $3 billion in business recoveries supported — filing in ACE through its own licensed brokers under its own filer code, and running the three lanes the firm describes as tariff refunds and drawback, vendor tariff recovery, and supplier recovery. CargoTrans runs the same work on its Captain platform. The pitch writes itself: the ruling is settled, the money is owed, and the only thing between an importer and its refund is a declaration it has never filed before.

The counterargument

There is a case for saying this market is already over. By October 2, $126 billion of the $166 billion had been sent to the Treasury for payment. The big importers, who hold most of the money, have largely been paid. The remaining pool is the hard tail — finally liquidated entries, missing bank data, litigated cases — and hard tails are where contingency fees get expensive and timelines get long. A recovery firm that built its business on IEEPA refunds in the spring of 2026 may find that by the spring of 2027 there is little left to recover.

That case is mostly right about IEEPA and mostly wrong about the trade. The IEEPA pool was a one-time event, and it is draining. But it did something permanent: it taught about 330,000 importers that the duties they pay are not always the duties they owe, and it taught them that the refund is a filing, not a favor. The firms that did the IEEPA work now hold the client relationships, the entry records, and the filing infrastructure — and the permanent refund business, duty drawback, runs on exactly those assets. Drawback returns 99 cents on the dollar with a five-year lookback, and roughly four-fifths of the money it could return is never claimed, at about $15 billion a year. The IEEPA windfall was the introduction. Drawback is the relationship.

There is a second permanent effect, and it is the one that matters most to anyone buying into this trade. The IEEPA episode changed what importers expect from their brokers and their lawyers. Before 2026, a customs broker was a filer — someone who got the goods through. After 2026, a customs broker is also a recovery agent, someone who watches liquidation dates and files protests inside 180 days and knows which duties are recoverable and which are not. The firms that understood that shift early — RSK, CargoTrans, and the rest — are now selling a service that did not exist as a category two years ago. The refund market is not the $166 billion. The refund market is the expectation, now established, that the money comes back.

For the importer in the opening scene, the wire has presumably cleared by now — CAPE pays in 60 to 90 days, and she filed early. For the importers who have not filed, the lesson is sharper: the ruling was the easy part. The refund is a declaration, a bank account on file, and a deadline met. The Court gave them the right. The paperwork is what gets them the money.
