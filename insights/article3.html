The Hundred-and-Eighty-Day Clock

A customs protest must be filed within 180 days of liquidation. There are no extensions, no waivers, and no second look. The clock, not the merits, decides most refund fights.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 18, 2026

The importer's lawyer found the problem on a Tuesday, in the third week of October, and the problem was not the duty. The duty was wrong — the entry had been classified under the wrong heading, and the difference was worth six figures on a single shipment. The problem was the date on the liquidation notice. The entry had liquidated in April. The 180-day clock had run out in October. The merits of the case were, by then, irrelevant. The liquidation was final, and the refund was gone — not because the importer was wrong, but because the importer was late.

That is the customs protest in one scene. A protest is the importer's challenge to a Customs decision — a classification, a valuation, a rate — and it is filed under 19 U.S.C. § 1514, on CBP Form 19 or electronically through the ACE Portal. The statute gives the importer 180 calendar days from the date of liquidation to file it. The deadline is jurisdictional: no extensions, no waivers, no equitable tolling, no good-cause exception. Miss it by one day and the liquidation stands, however wrong the underlying decision was. The filing cutoff is 11:59 p.m. Eastern on the 180th day, and the one softening in the rule is a small one — if the 180th day falls on a weekend or a federal holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day under 19 CFR 174.12(e).

The clock runs from the liquidation posting date, not from the day the importer learns about it. That distinction is the trap. Liquidation is the final computation of the duties owed on an entry, and it typically lands about 314 days after entry under CBP's standard cycle, set out at 19 CFR 159.11. CBP has one year from entry to liquidate under 19 U.S.C. § 1504; if it does not, the entry liquidates by operation of law at the rate the importer declared. Either way, the liquidation is posted, the clock starts, and no one sends the importer a personal reminder. The importer who does not watch the posting date discovers the deadline the way the lawyer in the opening scene did — after it has passed.

The protest itself is a modest filing with an outsized reach. A single protest may cover multiple entries at the same port, so long as the issues are substantially identical under 19 CFR 174.13(b) — which means one well-drafted protest can protect a year's worth of entries on the same question. The protest can also ask for further review, which moves the case to CBP headquarters when the issue is bigger than a single entry — a classification question that affects a whole product line, for example, or a valuation method that the importer uses across its business. CBP then has up to two years from filing to allow or deny the protest under 19 U.S.C. § 1515(a). If it denies the protest, expressly or by letting the two years run, the importer has another 180 days to file a civil action in the U.S. Court of International Trade.

Why timing is the product

For a recovery firm or a customs lawyer, the 180-day clock is not a procedural footnote. It is the product. The firms that do this work well do not sell legal brilliance; they sell a calendar. They watch liquidation dates for their clients, they file protests inside the window, and they keep the client's entries alive long enough for the merits to matter. The firms that do it badly — or not at all — sell the same service after the window has closed, which is to say they sell nothing.

The Section 122 surcharge made the point in 2026. The ten-percent surcharge was imposed on February 24, 2026, and on May 7, 2026, the Court of International Trade held in State of Washington v. Trump, Slip Op. 26-47, that the surcharge was unlawful — but limited the injunction to the three plaintiffs in the case. Every other importer had to use the standard protest pathway under § 1514: file within 180 days of liquidation, or lose the refund. The ruling was good news for three companies and a deadline for everyone else. That is the shape of the customs-claims business in a sentence: the law gives you the right, and the clock gives you the window.

The same logic runs through the IEEPA refunds. The Supreme Court's February 20, 2026 decision in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump made about $166 billion refundable, but the refunds came back through CBP's CAPE process, and CAPE runs on declarations filed in phases — unliquidated entries first, then entries within 80 days of liquidation, then, from October 6, 2026, the finally liquidated entries in Phase III. The importers who filed early were paid early; the importers who waited are now in the hard tail, waiting on Phase III or on the more than 3,700 refund suits filed at the Court of International Trade. The ruling was the same for all of them. The timing was not.

The counterargument

There is a case for saying the clock matters less than it used to. The IEEPA refunds showed that when the stakes are large enough, Customs builds a process — CAPE — that lets importers recover duties without filing a protest at all. If the next tariff fight ends the same way, with a dedicated refund process rather than a protest deadline, the 180-day clock becomes a backstop rather than the main event. A recovery firm that builds its whole practice on protest timing is betting that the next fight will look like the last one.

That bet is reasonable, but it understates what the clock does even when a CAPE-style process exists. CAPE's phases are themselves deadlines — Phase 1 covered unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation, and Phase III opened only in October 2026 for the finally liquidated entries. The importers who missed the early phases did not lose their refunds, but they lost months of time and, in many cases, the use of the money. And outside IEEPA, there is no CAPE. The ordinary customs dispute — a classification, a valuation, a rate — still runs on § 1514, and § 1514 still gives you 180 days and nothing more. The protest clock is not a bet on the next tariff fight. It is the standing rule for every fight that is not a tariff fight, which is to say, most of them.

What that means for the buyer of a name like this one is straightforward. The customs-claims trade is a trade in deadlines, and the deadline that matters most is the one that cannot be extended. A firm that owns the name, watches the liquidation dates, and files inside the window is selling the one thing an importer cannot buy after the fact: time. The importer in the opening scene had the merits, the documents, and the money — and lost all three to a calendar. The firms that prevent that loss are the ones importers pay.
