The thesis

A name for the refund business customs built.

Two refund machines run inside U.S. customs law, and both of them pay out on paperwork. The first is permanent: duty drawback, in the statute since the Tariff Act of 1789, returns up to 99 percent of the duties, taxes, and fees paid on imported goods that are later exported or destroyed, with a five-year lookback from the date of import under 19 U.S.C. § 1313. The second is a one-time event: on February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court held 6–3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize tariffs, and about $166 billion collected under that authority became refundable — with interest — through Customs and Border Protection's CAPE process.

The scale of the first machine is easy to understate. CBP refunds roughly $1 billion a year through drawback, and industry estimates put the eligible money left unclaimed at about $15 billion a year — roughly four-fifths of what the program could return. The importers who leave it behind are not small: about 330,000 importers remitted the $166 billion in IEEPA duties across more than 53 million entries. The money sits in the records of companies that import and export every day, and it expires entry by entry as the five-year window rolls forward.

The second machine is the one with the deadline attached. CAPE — the Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries — opened on April 20, 2026. By April 26, importers had already submitted about 75,300 CAPE declarations covering more than 11.2 million entries. By October 2, CBP had accepted about $136.6 billion in claims and sent about $126 billion to the Treasury for payment. The easy money moved fast. What is left falls into the hard cases: entries that were finally liquidated before anyone sued, importers whose bank details CBP does not hold — about $1.3 billion sat on hold in mid-September for that reason alone — and companies waiting on class actions that have not reached the merits. More than 3,700 refund suits have been filed at the Court of International Trade.

Exact, and built for a reachable buyer pool. The buyers are nameable: the licensed customs brokers who file in ACE, the contingency-fee recovery firms that already run this work — RSK, CargoTrans, Dutywise, DrawbackHQ — and the customs law firms that litigate protests and CIT cases. They all search the same two words. This domain is those two words, in order, with nothing added.
